What Should Be Checked Before Plant Start-Up?

The days before introducing hydrocarbons, chemicals or energy into a new or modified plant are among the most hazardous in its life. This checklist sets out what should be confirmed, with evidence, before start-up.

Why start-up deserves special attention

Start-up combines unusual operating conditions, equipment that has never run in service, temporary arrangements, many people on site and schedule pressure. Industry incident histories show a disproportionate share of serious events during start-up and other transient operations. A disciplined pre-start-up check, usually formalised as a pre-start-up safety review (PSSR), is the last line of defence against problems that earlier stages missed.

The checks below apply to new plants and, scaled appropriately, to significant modifications and restarts after turnarounds.

1. Design and hazard studies

  • The facility has been built to the approved design, and field changes have been through management of change
  • HAZOP, LOPA and other study recommendations are closed, or formally deferred with justification and approval
  • Relief and flare systems match the as-built design and current relief calculations
  • Process safety information (P&IDs, data sheets, cause and effect diagrams) reflects the as-built plant

2. Mechanical completion and integrity

  • Mechanical completion certificates are signed for the systems being started
  • Pressure and leak tests are complete and recorded
  • Temporary blinds, strainers, supports and test equipment have been removed or are controlled by a register
  • Line-ups have been checked against the P&IDs, and valves are in their start-up positions
  • Insulation, supports and bolting are complete where they affect safety or integrity

3. Safety systems

  • Safety instrumented functions have been tested end to end, from sensor to final element
  • Bypasses and overrides are recorded, approved and minimised
  • Fire and gas detection and alarm systems are tested and in service
  • Firewater, deluge and fixed suppression systems are available
  • Emergency shutdown, isolation and depressurisation systems are tested
  • Escape routes, emergency lighting and safety showers are complete and unobstructed

4. Utilities and supporting systems

  • Power, instrument air, nitrogen, cooling water, steam and fuel gas are available and stable
  • Control system configuration is complete, alarms are rationalised and set points are verified
  • Drains, vents and effluent systems are ready to receive start-up material
  • Communications and permit-to-work arrangements cover the new area

5. Procedures

  • Start-up, normal operating, shutdown and emergency procedures are approved and available where they are used
  • The first start-up procedure has been walked through in the field with the people who will use it
  • Procedures for any temporary start-up arrangements have been prepared and approved

6. People and competence

  • Operators and maintenance technicians are trained and assessed on the new or changed equipment
  • Shift staffing for start-up is defined, including extra support and vendor representatives
  • Roles and authority for start-up decisions are clear, including who can stop the start-up
  • Contractors on site have been briefed on start-up hazards and restrictions

7. Maintenance readiness

  • Equipment is registered in the maintenance system with preventive routines loaded
  • Critical spares and consumables are available on site
  • Lubricants, chemicals and catalysts are in place and correctly labelled

8. Punch list and change management

  • Punch items are categorised; those that affect safe start-up are closed
  • Remaining items have owners, dates and agreed controls
  • All management of change records related to the scope are closed or controlled

9. Emergency preparedness

  • The emergency response plan covers the new facility and its specific hazards
  • Emergency services and neighbours have been informed where required
  • A drill or table-top exercise has been held for credible start-up scenarios

10. Authorisation

  • The PSSR team has reviewed the evidence, not just the checklist, and recorded its findings
  • A start-up authorisation is signed by the person with the authority to give it
  • Care, custody and control has formally transferred to operations

Making the checks meaningful

The biggest weakness in many pre-start-up reviews is accepting statuses without evidence. A checked box that says “SIS tested” should point to a signed test record. A walk-down by experienced people, in the field, finds what document reviews miss. And readiness that was never planned cannot be created in the last week: the organisational items in sections 5 to 7 depend on operational readiness work that should have started during FEED.
