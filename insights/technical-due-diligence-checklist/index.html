Technical Due Diligence Checklist for Industrial Assets

Technical due diligence turns engineering facts into investment terms. This checklist sets out what to request, what to review and which findings typically change a valuation or a transaction structure.

Start with the investment thesis

Technical due diligence is most useful when it is focused on what drives value in the deal. Before issuing a data request, agree with the deal team which assumptions matter most: throughput, availability, efficiency, remaining life, sustaining CAPEX, operating cost or expansion potential. The checklist below is comprehensive; a red flag review would prioritise a subset.

Data room requests

  • Process and technology description, block flow diagrams, P&IDs and layouts
  • Design basis and nameplate capacities, with any de-ratings or upgrades since
  • Production, availability and efficiency data for at least three to five years
  • Maintenance history, major failures and root cause analyses
  • Inspection plans and reports, integrity and corrosion assessments
  • Turnaround and outage history and the next planned events
  • Sustaining and growth CAPEX plans, with supporting studies
  • OPEX budgets and actuals, maintenance and contractor costs
  • HSE statistics, incident reports, process safety studies and audit findings
  • Permits, licences, statutory inspection certificates and regulatory correspondence
  • Key contracts: offtake, feedstock, O&M, long-term service agreements, warranties
  • Organisation charts, staffing levels and key-person dependencies
  • Insurance surveys and recommendations

Review areas

Technology and design

Is the technology proven at this scale? Are there known design weaknesses, obsolete equipment or single points of failure? Are spare parts and vendor support still available?

Condition and integrity

What is the physical condition of critical equipment? Is the inspection programme adequate and up to date? Are there open anomalies, temporary repairs or overdue inspections? See asset integrity.

Operating performance

How do throughput, availability and efficiency compare with design and with similar assets? Are losses well understood? Is the performance used in the business plan supported by history?

Maintenance and reliability

Is maintenance keeping pace with need, or has work been deferred to improve short-term results? Is the backlog under control? Are bad actors known and being addressed?

CAPEX and OPEX

Are sustaining CAPEX estimates realistic in scope, cost and timing? Are major overhauls and replacements included? Are operating costs sustainable, or depressed by deferred maintenance?

HSE and process safety

What is the incident record? Are major accident hazards identified and controlled? Are hazard studies current, and are their actions closed?

Permits and compliance

Are all permits current? Are there pending regulatory changes, such as emissions limits, that will require investment?

Energy transition exposure

How do emissions and energy intensity compare with peers? What will it cost to meet expected regulatory or customer requirements?

Site visit priorities

A site visit tests what documents cannot. Look at housekeeping and the condition of critical equipment, talk to operators and maintenance staff, review the control room and alarm load, and compare what you see with the data room. A well-prepared visit focuses on the issues that the document review raised.

Red flags that move valuations

Common findings that change the deal

  • Significant deferred maintenance or a growing backlog
  • Overdue statutory inspections or unresolved integrity anomalies
  • Performance in the business plan that history does not support
  • Sustaining CAPEX that omits major overhauls or replacements
  • Obsolete control systems without a funded replacement plan
  • Process safety studies that are outdated or have many open actions
  • Permit conditions that will require major investment
  • Heavy dependence on a few individuals or on a single vendor

Turning findings into deal terms

Each material finding should be expressed in terms the deal team can use: a CAPEX or OPEX adjustment, a change to performance assumptions, a specific warranty or indemnity, a condition precedent, or an item for the post-acquisition plan. Findings without a quantified consequence are hard to act on.
