From FEED to Operations: Industrial Project Lifecycle Explained

Industrial projects follow a recognisable lifecycle from first idea to stable operation. Understanding what each stage must deliver, and why early decisions matter so much, helps owners, engineers and investors ask the right questions at the right time.

The stages at a glance

Stage Main question Typical deliverables
Feasibility Is there a viable project? Business case, technology options, order-of-magnitude cost
Concept (select) Which option is best? Option comparison, selected concept, basis of design, concept HAZID
FEED (define) Is the selected option defined well enough to commit? Design package, cost estimate and schedule, safety studies, contract strategy
Final investment decision Do we commit the capital? Approved budget, schedule and execution plan
EPC execution Design, procure and build Detailed design, procurement, construction
Commissioning and start-up Does it work, and can it be operated safely? Tested systems, PSSR, start-up
Handover and operations Is the organisation running the asset as intended? Performance tests, handover dossiers, stable operation

Many owners use a stage-gate process, where each stage ends with a formal decision to proceed, recycle or stop.

Why early stages matter most

The ability to influence a project’s cost and value is greatest at the start and falls as the design is fixed and money is committed. A change made during concept costs little; the same change during construction can cost many times more and delay start-up. Most of the project’s eventual problems, such as an unclear scope, unrealistic schedule, missing operations input or poor contract strategy, are created early, even if they only become visible later.

Feasibility and concept

The focus is on choosing the right project: confirming the business need, comparing technologies and locations, and identifying showstoppers. A concept HAZID at this stage identifies hazards that could rule out an option. Owners often underinvest here, then pay for it later.

FEED

Front-end engineering design develops the selected concept to the point where the project can be tendered and a final investment decision made. A good FEED package defines process design, major equipment, layout, utilities, the safety philosophy and key studies, together with a cost estimate and schedule of known accuracy. An independent FEED review before the investment decision tests whether the package is really ready.

FEED is also the time to set the operating and maintenance philosophies and to start operational readiness planning.

Final investment decision

The final investment decision commits the capital. Decision-makers need confidence in the scope, estimate, schedule, risks and execution plan. Weak FEED packages lead to optimistic decisions and later overruns.

EPC execution and construction

In engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) execution, the contractor completes detailed design, buys equipment and builds the facility. The owner’s role changes from developing the design to overseeing delivery. An owner’s engineer protects the owner’s interests in design quality, change management, construction quality and progress verification, while project management and controls keep scope, schedule and cost under control.

Key activities include the detailed design HAZOP, vendor package reviews, factory acceptance tests, construction quality surveillance and interface management.

Commissioning, start-up and handover

Commissioning proves that systems work, the pre-start-up safety review confirms readiness for safe start-up, and performance tests demonstrate that the plant meets its guarantees. Handover transfers care, custody and control, along with the as-built documentation and data the operator needs for decades.

Operations

The project only delivers value once the facility runs reliably. The early months of operation reveal design weaknesses, procedure gaps and early-life equipment failures. Planned support during ramp-up, followed by sound asset management, protects the value the project was meant to create.

Where projects commonly go wrong

  • Scope not frozen before FEED is completed
  • Cost estimates presented with more accuracy than the design maturity supports
  • Operations and maintenance input missing from design reviews
  • Contract strategy that does not match the owner’s capacity to manage it
  • Interfaces between contractors and with existing facilities poorly managed
  • Commissioning planned too late and treated as part of construction
  • Handover data and documentation left until the end

Where independent specialists add value

Owners do not need large permanent teams for every stage. Independent specialists can review FEED before investment decisions, act as owner’s engineers during execution, run health checks when projects drift, and support commissioning and readiness. Investors use the same expertise for technical due diligence. The right specialist at the right stage is usually far cheaper than fixing a problem one stage later.
