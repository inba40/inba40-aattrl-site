What Is a HAZOP Study and When Is It Required?

A HAZOP is the most widely used detailed hazard study in the process industries. This guide explains what it is, how it works, when it is required and the practical reasons some studies deliver far more value than others.

The short answer

A hazard and operability study (HAZOP) is a structured, team-based review of a process design or operation. The team divides the plant into sections called nodes, defines what each node is meant to do, and then uses guide words to imagine how it could deviate from that intent. For every credible deviation the team records the causes, the consequences, the existing safeguards and any recommendations.

A HAZOP is required whenever a regulation, a company standard or a project specification calls for a detailed hazard analysis of a process, which in practice means most new process facilities, significant modifications and periodic revalidations of operating plants.

Where the method came from

HAZOP was developed at ICI in the United Kingdom in the 1960s and spread across the chemical, oil and gas industries in the following decades. The international application guide is IEC 61882. Most operators and engineering contractors also have their own HAZOP procedures that set out the risk matrix, the recording format and the rules for recommendations.

How a HAZOP works

The core of the method is the combination of guide words with process parameters such as flow, pressure, temperature, level and composition.

Guide word Meaning Example deviation
No / none Complete negation of the intent No flow
More Quantitative increase More pressure, higher temperature
Less Quantitative decrease Less flow, lower level
As well as Something additional present Contamination, a second phase
Part of Something missing Missing component in a mixture
Reverse Logical opposite of the intent Reverse flow
Other than Complete substitution Wrong material, unplanned operating mode

For batch and sequential operations, guide words such as early, late, before and after examine the timing and order of steps.

The team follows each consequence to its worst credible outcome assuming the safeguards fail, then considers whether the safeguards are adequate. This discipline is what makes HAZOP effective: it forces the team to test assumptions rather than accept them. Scenarios with severe consequences are often taken forward to layer of protection analysis to test the safeguards more rigorously.

When is a HAZOP required?

Regulatory drivers

  • In the United States, OSHA’s process safety management standard requires a process hazard analysis for covered processes, using HAZOP or another appropriate method, and requires it to be updated and revalidated at least every five years.
  • In Europe, major accident regulations based on the Seveso III Directive, such as COMAH in Great Britain, require operators of upper-tier establishments to demonstrate that major accident hazards have been identified and controlled. HAZOP is a common way of doing this.
  • In India and the GCC, requirements typically come through national rules for hazardous installations, the standards of bodies such as India’s Oil Industry Safety Directorate, and the specifications of national oil companies and operators.

Project and operating triggers

Stage Typical HAZOP Notes
Late FEED or early detailed design Main design HAZOP P&IDs mature enough to review, early enough to change
Vendor package finalisation Package HAZOP Once vendor P&IDs and control philosophy are available
Construction HAZOP of changes Significant design changes go through MOC and may need a study
Operations Modification HAZOP Triggered by the management of change process
Operations Revalidation Often every five years, or as the regulator or company requires
Decommissioning Procedural HAZOP Of the decommissioning and cleaning procedures

What a HAZOP is not

A HAZOP is not a design review, and it should not be used to check whether drawings are correct; that is the job of the design team and design assurance reviewers. It does not quantify risk the way a quantitative risk assessment does. And the workshop is not the place to solve every problem: the team identifies issues and agrees what must be done, then engineers develop solutions afterwards.

Why some HAZOPs disappoint

  • Drawings not frozen. The design keeps changing during or after the study, so the record no longer matches the plant.
  • The wrong team. Without operations and instrumentation representatives, the team misses how the plant will actually be run and controlled.
  • Marathon sessions. Long days lead to fatigue and superficial review of later nodes.
  • Vague recommendations. Actions such as “review design” cannot be closed objectively.
  • Actions never closed. The register is filed and forgotten, or items are marked complete without verification.

Each of these can be avoided with preparation, an experienced independent facilitator and a disciplined action close-out process.

How long does a HAZOP take?

Duration depends on the number and complexity of nodes, the quality of preparation and the format. A small modification can take a day; a new process unit can take several weeks. Online studies usually run three to four hours a day to keep the team effective. A facilitator can give a realistic estimate once they have seen the P&IDs.

Where HAZOP fits with other studies

A HAZID identifies major hazards earlier, at concept and FEED stage. LOPA follows the HAZOP for high-consequence scenarios. Before start-up, the pre-start-up safety review checks that HAZOP actions have been closed. For a direct comparison, read HAZID vs HAZOP: What Is the Difference?
