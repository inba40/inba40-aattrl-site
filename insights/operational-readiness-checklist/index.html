Operational Readiness Checklist for New Industrial Facilities

Operational readiness work turns a completed project into a working operation. This checklist sets out what an owner should have in place at each phase, so that people, procedures, systems and spares are ready when the plant is.

How to use this checklist

The checklist is organised by project phase because timing is the main reason readiness fails. Recruitment, training, maintenance data and spares all have long lead times, and each phase depends on the one before. Adapt the items to the size of the facility and the maturity of the owner’s organisation: a new operator building its first plant needs far more than an established operator adding a unit to an existing site.

FEED: set the foundations

  • Operating philosophy agreed: operating modes, staffing concept, shift pattern, level of automation
  • Maintenance philosophy agreed: in-house versus contracted work, maintenance concepts by equipment type
  • Owner’s organisation designed, including the readiness team and its leader
  • Operations and maintenance representatives assigned to design reviews and hazard studies
  • Readiness plan and budget included in the project execution plan and the cost estimate
  • Asset information requirements defined so that the contractor delivers usable data

Detailed design: build the plan

  • Readiness plan integrated with the project schedule, including commissioning and handover milestones
  • Recruitment plan with dates for key roles, allowing time to train before commissioning
  • Training needs analysis completed for each role
  • Equipment criticality assessment started as the equipment list stabilises
  • Spare parts strategy agreed, with capital and long-lead spares identified for early purchase
  • Procedure list developed and writing responsibilities assigned
  • Operations reviews of design for operability and maintainability completed

Construction: prepare the organisation

  • Key operations and maintenance staff recruited and mobilised
  • Training delivered: fundamentals, process, equipment, vendor and, where used, simulator training
  • Competence assurance in place for safety-critical roles
  • Operating and maintenance procedures written and reviewed
  • Maintenance system configured: asset hierarchy, equipment master data, maintenance plans and task lists
  • Operating spares ordered, received and catalogued
  • Business systems ready: permit to work, document control, laboratory, inventory, reporting
  • Contracts in place for specialist services and long-term vendor support

Commissioning: take part and take over

  • Operators and technicians working alongside the commissioning team on their own systems
  • Procedures validated in the field and updated
  • Handover sequence and acceptance criteria agreed between project, commissioning and operations
  • As-built documentation and vendor data received in the agreed format and checked
  • Maintenance plans activated as systems are handed over
  • Readiness reviews held before each major handover and before the pre-start-up safety review

Start-up and ramp-up: stabilise

  • Additional support on shift for the first weeks of operation
  • Early-life failures tracked and investigated
  • Procedure and training gaps captured and closed
  • Maintenance backlog and spares usage monitored
  • Lessons learned recorded for the next project

Elements that are often missed

  • Maintenance data quality. A maintenance system loaded at the last minute, from incomplete vendor data, will drive poor maintenance for years.
  • Capital spares. Critical, long-lead items such as large motors or compressor rotors may take a year or more to obtain.
  • Interfaces with existing sites. Expansions inherit procedures and systems that may not fit the new facility.
  • Readiness leadership. Readiness without a named, empowered leader tends to be squeezed out by project priorities.
  • Ramp-up period. Plans often stop at start-up, but the first months of operation are when gaps appear.

Measuring readiness

A simple maturity rating for each element, reviewed at project gates, makes readiness visible to leadership. Ratings should be backed by evidence: recruited headcount against plan, training completion, procedures approved, maintenance plans loaded, spares received. An independent assessment, typically a year or more before start-up, gives an honest view while there is still time to act.
