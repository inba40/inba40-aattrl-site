What is a HAZID?

A hazard identification study (HAZID) is a structured workshop that identifies the major hazards of a project or facility early, while there is still freedom to change the concept, layout or execution plan. Instead of reviewing detailed drawings line by line, the team works through a list of hazard categories, such as process hazards, fire and explosion, toxic release, natural hazards, external threats, transport, construction, health, environment and security, and asks which apply, what could go wrong and how each hazard is or will be managed.

ISO 17776 describes hazard identification and risk assessment for offshore production installations, and its approach is widely used onshore. Many operators maintain their own HAZID guide-word sets tailored to their assets.

Types of HAZID

Concept HAZID

Held during concept selection to compare options and identify hazards that could rule an option out, such as proximity to populations, simultaneous operations or large toxic inventories. The output often shapes the basis of design and the safety philosophy.

FEED HAZID

Revisits the selected concept as the design develops, with plot plans and process flow diagrams available. It confirms the major accident hazards that will drive layout, spacing, fire protection and escape routes, and lists the studies needed later, such as HAZOP, fire and explosion risk assessment or quantitative risk assessment.

Construction HAZID

Focuses on how the facility will be built: heavy lifts, work at height, excavation near live services, simultaneous construction and operations, logistics and the transition into commissioning.

Operations HAZID

Applies to operating assets, for example before a change in operating philosophy, after an acquisition, or when a safety case is prepared or updated. It draws on operating experience and incident history rather than design assumptions.

Brownfield HAZID

Covers modifications to live plants, where the main hazards often sit at the interfaces: hot work near hydrocarbons, tie-ins, temporary equipment, and the effect of the change on existing escape routes and emergency systems.

How a HAZID workshop runs

  1. Agree the scope, objectives, guide-word list and risk matrix.
  2. Brief the team on the facility, its location and the project stage.
  3. Work through each area or system against the guide words, recording hazards, causes, consequences, safeguards and recommendations.
  4. Rank the hazards and identify the major accident hazards.
  5. Review the records with the team, then issue the report and action register.

Who should attend

A HAZID benefits from a broader team than a HAZOP: project management, process, technical safety, operations, construction and environmental specialists and, where relevant, logistics, security and the client’s HSE function. An experienced facilitator keeps the discussion at the right level, identifying hazards and the studies they need rather than solving every engineering detail in the room.

Information required

  • Project description and basis of design
  • Plot plan and site data: surroundings, nearby populations, access, meteorology
  • Process flow diagrams and inventories of hazardous materials
  • Execution and construction strategy, for construction HAZIDs
  • Previous hazard studies, incident history and the operator’s HAZID procedure

Deliverables

  • HAZID worksheets and a register of identified hazards
  • List of major accident hazards with their risk ranking
  • Action register with owners and due dates
  • List of follow-on studies, for example HAZOP, quantitative risk assessment (QRA), fire and explosion risk assessment (FERA) or escape, evacuation and rescue assessment (EERA)
  • A HAZID report suitable for client, lender and regulator review

Why use an independent HAZID facilitator?

An external facilitator brings experience of comparable facilities, a tested guide-word set and the independence to challenge assumptions made by the design team. With a dedicated scribe, the workshop stays efficient and the records support later safety cases, permits and audits. For projects with several contractors, an independent chair also helps make sure interface hazards are not left between scopes.

Frequently asked questions

When should a HAZID be carried out?

At concept selection, again during FEED as the design develops, before construction starts, and before significant changes to an operating asset. Early HAZIDs have the greatest influence because the layout and concept can still change.

How long does a HAZID take?

Most project HAZIDs take one to three days. Large multi-area facilities, or combined construction and operations HAZIDs, can take longer.

Is a HAZID the same as a HAZOP?

No. A HAZID is broad and early, using hazard categories to identify major hazards. A HAZOP is detailed, using guide words on P&IDs to examine process deviations node by node. Most process projects need both.

Can a HAZID be held online?

Yes. Online HAZIDs work well with good preparation and shorter sessions. Construction and brownfield HAZIDs often benefit from at least part of the team being on site.

Who owns the HAZID actions?

The project or asset owner. Each action should have a named owner and due date; AATTRL can support close-out reviews.
