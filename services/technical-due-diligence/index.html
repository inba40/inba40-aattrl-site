Who technical due diligence is for

For investors and lenders, technical due diligence (TDD) answers a simple question: is the asset what the seller says it is, and what will it cost to keep it that way? Findings are expressed in terms that feed the valuation and the transaction documents: CAPEX and OPEX adjustments, risks for warranties and indemnities, and conditions for financing.

For engineers and management teams, TDD is an independent technical review of condition, performance, integrity, compliance and plans. A seller may commission vendor due diligence to prepare for a sale; a buyer commissions buy-side due diligence; a lender commissions an independent engineer’s review.

What we review

Area Key questions
Technology and design Is the technology proven at this scale? Are there design weaknesses or obsolescence?
Condition and integrity What is the physical condition? Are inspection and integrity programmes adequate?
Operating performance How do availability, throughput, efficiency and quality compare with design and peers?
Maintenance and reliability Is maintenance keeping pace? Is there deferred work or a backlog that hides cost?
CAPEX plan Are sustaining and growth CAPEX realistic in scope, cost and timing?
OPEX Are operating and maintenance costs reasonable and sustainable?
HSE and process safety What is the incident record? Are major hazards managed? Are there regulatory issues?
Permits and compliance Are permits, licences and statutory inspections current?
Projects under construction Are schedule, budget, contracts and contractor performance on track?
Energy transition exposure How do emissions, energy intensity and decarbonisation needs affect future cost and value?

Red flag or full due diligence

  • Red flag review: a fast, focused review, often in one to two weeks, highlighting issues that could stop the deal or change its value.
  • Full technical due diligence: a comprehensive review with site visits and management sessions, typically in three to six weeks depending on asset complexity and data quality.

How a TDD runs

  1. Scope and data request. Agree priorities with the deal team and issue a focused information request.
  2. Data room review. Analyse technical documents, performance data, maintenance records, CAPEX plans and reports.
  3. Management sessions and site visit. Test findings with management and inspect the asset.
  4. Quantify. Express findings as cost, schedule, performance or risk impacts.
  5. Report. Deliver findings in a structure that feeds the financial model and transaction documents.

Deliverables

  • Red flag report or full TDD report with an executive summary for the investment committee
  • CAPEX and OPEX review with recommended adjustments
  • Risk register with likelihood, impact and suggested mitigations
  • Input to transaction documents and conditions precedent
  • Optional 100-day plan for the new owner

Information required

Typical data room content: technical descriptions and drawings, operating and maintenance records, inspection reports, incident records, CAPEX and OPEX budgets, permits and contracts. A practical list is in our Technical Due Diligence Checklist for Industrial Assets.

TDD findings often lead to an owner’s engineer role during completion of a project, or to asset integrity work after acquisition.

Frequently asked questions

How long does technical due diligence take?

A red flag review typically takes one to two weeks. A full technical due diligence usually takes three to six weeks, depending on asset complexity, data availability and site access.

Can you work under strict confidentiality?

Yes. Engagements can be covered by a non-disclosure agreement, and specialists are checked for conflicts of interest with the parties involved before being proposed.

Do you provide reliance letters to lenders?

Reliance arrangements depend on the engagement, the specialists’ qualifications and insurance, and the lender’s requirements. They are agreed case by case before work begins.

Can you review a project still under construction?

Yes. Reviews of projects in development or construction cover design maturity, contracts, schedule, budget and contractor performance.
