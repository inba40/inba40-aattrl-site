What is HAZOP?

A hazard and operability study (HAZOP) is a structured, team-based review of a process design or operation. The team works through the facility one section at a time, called a node, and asks how the process could deviate from its design intent, what would cause each deviation, what the consequences would be and whether the safeguards are adequate. Where they are not, the team records a recommendation.

The method was developed in the chemical industry in the 1960s and is described in IEC 61882, the international application guide for HAZOP studies. It finds both safety hazards and operability problems, the issues that cause trips, off-specification product and difficult start-ups, which is why it remains the main detailed hazard study for process facilities.

When is HAZOP required?

Requirements depend on the jurisdiction, the regulator and the operator’s own standards. Process safety management rules in the United States (OSHA 29 CFR 1910.119) require a process hazard analysis for covered processes, and HAZOP is one of the accepted methods. Major accident hazard regimes based on the EU Seveso III Directive, such as COMAH in Great Britain, expect operators to demonstrate that hazards have been identified and controlled. Most international operators and EPC contractors also mandate HAZOP through company standards and project specifications, whether or not a regulation names it.

In practice a HAZOP is normally needed at four points.

New projects

The main design HAZOP is held when the piping and instrumentation diagrams (P&IDs) are developed enough to review but early enough to change, typically at the end of FEED or early in detailed design. A follow-up HAZOP of vendor packages is often held once vendor data is available.

Brownfield modifications

Changes to an operating plant, such as tie-ins, debottlenecking, new equipment or changed feedstock and operating conditions, usually need a HAZOP of the modified section and its interfaces, triggered through the site’s management of change (MOC) process. These studies are short but demand care at the boundaries between new and existing equipment.

Existing plants

Facilities that were never studied, or whose original HAZOP no longer reflects the plant after years of modifications, may need a full baseline HAZOP. This is common after an acquisition or when an operator harmonises standards across sites.

Revalidation

Many regulators and companies require the hazard analysis to be revalidated at a fixed interval, often every five years. A revalidation checks that the previous study still reflects the plant, covers the changes made since, considers incidents and near misses, and confirms that earlier recommendations were closed out. Depending on how much has changed it can be a focused review or a full study.

HAZOP methodology

  1. Agree scope and terms of reference. Boundaries, drawing set and revision, operating modes to cover (normal operation, start-up, shutdown, maintenance), the risk matrix and how recommendations will be ranked.
  2. Divide the design into nodes. Each node is a section with a clear design intent, such as a separator and its inlet, or a compressor and its suction system.
  3. Apply guide words to process parameters. Combinations such as no flow, more pressure, less level or reverse flow generate credible deviations from the design intent.
  4. Examine causes, consequences and safeguards. For each credible deviation the team identifies causes, follows the consequence to its worst credible outcome without safeguards, and lists the safeguards that prevent or mitigate it.
  5. Rank and recommend. Where risk is not adequately controlled, the team agrees a clear, actionable recommendation. Scenarios that need more rigorous analysis are flagged for LOPA and SIL assessment.
  6. Record and report. Every discussion is recorded in the worksheet in real time, reviewed by the team and issued with the report.

Experienced facilitators adapt the depth of analysis to the hazard. Utility systems and simple nodes move quickly; high-pressure, toxic or reactive systems receive the time they need.

Typical workshop team

A productive HAZOP team is small enough to stay focused and broad enough to understand both the design and how it will be operated.

Role Contribution
HAZOP facilitator (chair) Leads the method, manages time and quality, keeps the discussion objective
Scribe Records the worksheet live and tracks actions and open questions
Process engineer Explains design intent, process conditions and control philosophy
Operations representative Brings experience of how the plant is actually run and maintained
Instrument and control engineer Covers control loops, alarms, trips and the safety instrumented system
Technical or process safety engineer Advises on consequences, relief, fire and gas, and risk ranking

Mechanical, rotating equipment and vendor specialists join for the nodes where they are needed. Five to eight participants is usually the effective range.

Preparation requirements

Much of the quality of a HAZOP is decided before the workshop starts. The facilitator will typically request:

  • P&IDs at a frozen “issued for HAZOP” revision, plus process flow diagrams
  • Heat and material balance, process description and operating philosophy
  • Cause and effect diagrams, alarm and trip schedules, control narratives
  • Equipment data sheets, relief valve and flare data
  • Plot plan and layout drawings
  • Previous HAZID and HAZOP reports and the status of their actions
  • For existing plants: operating procedures, MOC records and incident history
  • The company risk matrix and HAZOP procedure, if one exists

A short pre-HAZOP meeting to confirm the nodes, attendance and drawing set prevents most delays.

Deliverables

  • HAZOP worksheets for every node, including deviations considered and judged not credible
  • A HAZOP report covering scope, method, team and attendance, nodes, assumptions and findings
  • Marked-up node drawings
  • A ranked recommendation list and the scenarios to be taken forward to LOPA
  • Optional support to review responses and close out actions

HAZOP action register

Recommendations only reduce risk when they are closed. Each action in the register has a unique number, a clear statement of what is required, the node and scenario it came from, a risk ranking, an owner and a target date. Responses are reviewed and accepted by a competent person rather than simply marked complete. AATTRL can set up the register, chair action review meetings and confirm that agreed changes have been made, which feeds directly into the pre-start-up safety review before commissioning.

Online, onsite and hybrid HAZOP

Online HAZOP

Remote HAZOPs work well when the team is spread across countries and the design is mature. They need shorter sessions, usually three to four hours a day, reliable connections, screen-shared worksheets and drawings, and firm facilitation. They avoid travel costs and make it easy to bring in a specialist for a single node.

Onsite HAZOP

Onsite workshops remain the better choice for complex new units, studies with many vendor packages, and revalidations where the team benefits from walking the plant. Face-to-face sessions typically allow five to six hours of active review per day. A hybrid format, with the core team in one room and specialists joining remotely, is often the practical compromise.

Why independent facilitation matters

A HAZOP is only as good as its challenge. A facilitator from the design contractor may be under schedule pressure or reluctant to question their own company’s design. An independent facilitator has no stake in the outcome, can push for evidence when the team assumes a safeguard will work, and can resolve disagreements between contractor and owner on technical merit. For operators, independent facilitation also gives confidence that the study will stand up to regulatory and insurer review.

AATTRL proposes facilitators whose experience matches the process, whether gas processing, LNG, refining, chemicals, power or utilities, so the team spends its time on hazards rather than explaining the plant.

Frequently asked questions

What is a HAZOP study?

A HAZOP (hazard and operability study) is a systematic team review of a process design or operation. Guide words applied to process parameters identify how the process can deviate from its design intent, the causes and consequences, and whether the safeguards are adequate.

Who should facilitate a HAZOP?

An experienced, independent facilitator who knows the method and the type of process, and who is not responsible for the design under review. The facilitator should be supported by a dedicated scribe so that recording does not slow the team.

When should a HAZOP be conducted?

When the P&IDs are mature but still changeable, usually late FEED or early detailed design. Further HAZOPs are held for vendor packages, plant modifications and the periodic revalidation of operating facilities.

How long does a HAZOP take?

It depends on the number and complexity of nodes and on preparation. A small modification can be reviewed in a day; a new process unit can take several weeks. The facilitator estimates the duration once the P&IDs and scope are available.

What information is required?

Frozen P&IDs, process flow diagrams, heat and material balance, cause and effect diagrams, equipment and relief data, layout drawings and previous hazard study reports. For existing plants, also procedures, MOC records and incident history.

Can HAZOP be conducted remotely?

Yes. Online HAZOPs work well with a mature design, good connectivity, shorter daily sessions and an experienced facilitator. Complex new units often still benefit from onsite or hybrid workshops.

What are typical HAZOP deliverables?

Completed worksheets, a HAZOP report, marked-up drawings, a ranked action register with owners and due dates, and a list of scenarios for LOPA. Action close-out support can be added.

What is the difference between HAZID and HAZOP?

HAZID is a broad, early identification of major hazards using hazard categories, usually at concept or FEED stage. HAZOP is a detailed, node-by-node review of the process design using P&IDs. Most projects need both; see HAZID vs HAZOP.
