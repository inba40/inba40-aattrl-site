What is operational readiness?

Operational readiness, sometimes called operations readiness and assurance (ORA), is the work that makes sure an organisation can safely and reliably operate and maintain a new facility from its first day in service. A plant can be mechanically complete, commissioned and technically sound and still struggle for months because the people, procedures, maintenance systems, spares and information needed to run it were not ready. Readiness planning closes that gap.

Why it matters

The symptoms of weak readiness are familiar: start-up delayed while procedures are written, maintenance run from spreadsheets because the maintenance system was never populated, missing spares for critical equipment, operators who watched commissioning but never operated, engineering documents that never reached the operator’s systems, and unclear responsibility at handover. Each one extends ramp-up, erodes early reliability and increases the chance of an incident.

When to start

Readiness should begin in FEED, when the operating and maintenance philosophies are set and the owner’s organisation is designed, and continue through construction, commissioning and the first months of operation. A late start is common. An assessment well before start-up can still recover a great deal, but some elements, such as recruiting and training a large workforce or procuring long-lead spares, need more time than a late assessment allows.

Readiness scope

Element What “ready” looks like
Organization Operating model, organisation chart, roles and interfaces agreed; service contracts in place
People Recruitment and mobilisation plan delivered; operators and maintainers in post in time to take part in commissioning
Procedures Operating, start-up, shutdown, emergency and maintenance procedures written, field-validated and approved
Maintenance Maintenance strategy and preventive routines developed for all equipment, based on criticality
CMMS Asset hierarchy, equipment master data, maintenance plans, task lists and spare parts lists loaded and tested
Spares Commissioning, capital and operating spares identified, ordered, received and catalogued
Training Training needs analysis completed; programmes delivered, including vendor and simulator training where relevant
Competency Competence assurance in place for safety-critical roles, with assessments recorded
Systems Permit to work, document control, historian, laboratory, inventory and reporting systems configured and used
Documentation As-built drawings, vendor data, manuals and process safety information handed over in usable form
Handover System handover sequence, acceptance criteria and transfer of care, custody and control agreed
Start-up readiness Readiness reviews and the pre-start-up safety review planned and resourced

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between operational readiness and commissioning?

Commissioning proves that the plant and its systems work. Operational readiness makes sure the organisation is ready to run and maintain them: people, procedures, maintenance systems, spares and information. Both are needed for a smooth start-up.

When should operational readiness start?

During FEED, when the operating and maintenance philosophies are set. Starting at mechanical completion is too late for recruitment, training and long-lead spares.

Who owns operational readiness?

The asset owner or future operator. It is usually led by a readiness manager in the owner’s team, working closely with the project and commissioning teams.

How long does an operational readiness assessment take?

A focused assessment of a single facility typically takes two to four weeks, including document review, interviews and a site visit.

Can you support readiness for an expansion of an existing plant?

Yes. Expansions have their own readiness risks, particularly at the interfaces with existing systems, procedures and maintenance plans.
